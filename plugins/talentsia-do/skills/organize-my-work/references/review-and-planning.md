# Review and planning

Read for a weekly review, strategic alignment review, or project that needs more than outcome and next-action clarification. These are review practices for the current system; they do not authorize new commitments.

## Weekly review

Aim for a weekly cadence, adjusted to actual needs. A missed review is a reason to resume, not to abandon the system. Use the three phases below, adapted from the [official Weekly Review](https://gettingthingsdone.com/2009/05/the-gtd-weekly-review/).

### Get clear

Gather loose inputs from accessible capture points; clarify and route the inbox; capture remaining concerns that the commitment owner has expressed. Inbox processing assigns meaning and destination rather than completing every item. Retain unresolved questions visibly.

### Get current

Update action lists and completion evidence. Look back through the calendar for missed follow-ups and ahead for preparation needs. Check outstanding results owed by others, project outcomes and next steps, and relevant recurring checklists. Identify stale records and dependencies; reconcile duplicates while preserving history. Check that each active outcome has a viable path forward.

### Get creative

Revisit incubated options and emerging opportunities. Recommend activation, renegotiation, or retirement when appropriate; the commitment owner decides new commitments and consequential portfolio changes.

Record the review date, sources covered, material gaps, consequential updates, and decisions still needed in the existing review record if one exists, or a concise record when useful. Do not manufacture a complete review when data is missing. Report a compact attention view, not all lists.

## Horizons of focus

Use the [official horizons model](https://gettingthingsdone.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/2016-Horizons-of-Focus.pdf) when priorities conflict or portfolio alignment needs examination:

| Horizon | Review question |
| --- | --- |
| Ground: actions and calendar | What can be done next, and what is fixed? |
| 1: projects | Which outcomes are currently committed? |
| 2: areas of focus and accountability | Which continuing responsibilities need to be maintained? |
| 3: goals and objectives | What results matter over roughly the next one to two years? |
| 4: vision | What longer-term future is the commitment owner aiming for? |
| 5: purpose and principles | Why pursue it, and which standards guide decisions? |

An ongoing area such as health or financial stewardship is not itself a completable project. Distinguish maintaining its standards from achieving a specific outcome. Use stated priorities and evidence; do not invent the commitment owner's values or turn each horizon into a mandatory database.

Assess whether active projects support these responsibilities and goals, whether workload is realistic, and what needs renegotiation. An available strategy adviser may advise when strategy warrants it; the system steward maintains the system and brings consequential choices to the commitment owner. Resolve those assignments from the workspace context. Review higher horizons when circumstances change or alignment is unclear, without imposing a rigid schedule.

## Natural planning

Use the [Natural Planning Model](https://gettingthingsdone.com/2024/11/the-natural-planning-model/) when a project's purpose, success criteria, or path is unclear:

1. Clarify why it matters and the principles or constraints governing it.
2. Describe observable success.
3. Generate possible approaches without treating ideas as commitments.
4. Group useful ideas into components, sequence, and dependencies.
5. Identify executable next actions and who should perform them.

Scale planning to the uncertainty. A simple project may need only an outcome and one action; a complex one may need supporting notes and parallel workstreams. Preserve speculative steps in support material and update the plan from results instead of building an exhaustive imagined task list.
